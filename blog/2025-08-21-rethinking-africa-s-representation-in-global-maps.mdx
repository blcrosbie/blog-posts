## Introduction

Africa is roughly three times the size of Europe, yet the world's most popular map, the Mercator projection, presents a distorted view of this reality. As part of a growing movement, the African Union has endorsed a campaign to replace the Mercator map with more accurate alternatives, such as the Equal Earth projection. This shift is not merely about cartography; it challenges long-standing perceptions and narratives about Africa's significance on the global stage.

## Problem Framing

The Mercator projection, designed by Gerardus Mercator in 1569, was intended for navigation. However, it distorts landmasses, particularly as they move away from the equator. For instance, Greenland appears nearly the same size as Africa, despite being 14 times smaller in reality. This misrepresentation fosters a perception that Africa is marginal, undermining its true dimensions and importance.

## System Model

The Equal Earth projection, proposed by Bernhard Jenny and his colleagues in 2018, aims to correct these distortions. Unlike the Mercator projection, it accurately represents countries and continents according to their actual surface area. This approach is crucial for educational and political narratives, as maps influence how we perceive geography and power dynamics.

### Key Features of Projections

| Feature               | Equal Earth Projection       | Mercator Projection          |
|-----------------------|------------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Surface Area**      | Accurate representation       | Distorted, particularly at poles |
| **Purpose**           | Global perspective            | Navigation                   |
| **Shape Preservation**| Moderate distortion           | High shape preservation       |

## Trade-offs

Choosing a map projection involves trade-offs. The Mercator projection excels in preserving angles and shapes, making it useful for navigation. However, it sacrifices accurate size representation, especially for regions like Africa and South America. In contrast, the Equal Earth projection offers a more truthful depiction of landmass sizes but may not be as effective for navigational purposes.

## Worked Example

Consider the implications of using these projections in education. If students learn from the Mercator map, they may underestimate Africa's size and significance. For instance, Africa's area is approximately 11.73 million square miles, yet it is often depicted as smaller than Europe. This discrepancy can shape perceptions and limit understanding of Africa's geopolitical importance.
